## Research: capability.md

**Headline.** About 127 grams of asteroid have ever been brought to Earth, for about $1.4 billion; no spacecraft has touched an asteroid as small or fast-spinning as a typical target, no company has reached one, and everything measured in tonnes exists only on paper.

Report written to docs/research/capability.md (348 lines, every number marked primary, secondary, recalled or computed). The arithmetic script is data/research/scripts/budget.py.

DONE. Three asteroid sample returns:
- Hayabusa (Itokawa, 2003 to 2010): over 1,500 dust particles, no mass recorded, usually given as under 1 mg; about ¥20 billion (https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22182).
- Hayabusa2 (Ryugu, 2014 to 2020): 5.424 g (https://curation.isas.jaxa.jp/en/sample-curation/ryugu/); ¥28.9 billion, a 2012 plan figure (https://www8.cao.go.jp/space/comittee/dai2/siryou2-17.pdf); about $35 to 48 million per gram.
- OSIRIS-REx (Bennu, 2016 to 2023): 121.6 g (https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/osiris-rex/2024/02/15/nasa-announces-osiris-rex-bulk-sample-mass/); $1.02 billion (https://www.planetary.org/space-policy/cost-of-osiris-rex); $8.4 million per gram.
Together about 127 g for about $1.4 billion, each six to seven years after launch. All three asteroids are hundreds of metres wide and turn in 4 to 12 hours. For scale: Apollo 382 kg at about $809,000 per gram in 2025 dollars; Chang'e-5 1,731 g; Chang'e-6 1,935.3 g. Platinum was $41.64 per gram on 21 November 2026 (https://www.kitco.com/charts/liveplatinum.html).

UNDER WAY. Tianwen-2 has been 20 km from Kamoʻoalewa (18 m, turns every 28 minutes) since July 2026 (https://www.cnsa.gov.cn/n6758823/n6758838/c10760422/content.html). Target about 100 g, capsule due late November 2027. No surface contact is reported; my newest source is 13 October. It is the first spacecraft to stay at an asteroid under 100 m. The smallest body ever touched is still Dimorphos (160 m, DART's impact). The only anchoring attempt on any small body, Philae, failed on all three mechanisms. OSIRIS-REx sank half a metre into Bennu and would have kept sinking.

COMMERCIAL. Nothing has reached an asteroid.
- AstroForge: Brokkr-1's refinery was never switched on ("Refinery Demonstration - NOT COMPLETED"); Odin (120 kg, $3.5 million) was lost days after its February 2025 launch; DeepSpace-2 (200 kg) is now 2027 and has narrowed from docking to "rendezvous and imaging" (https://www.astroforge.com/deepspace-2).
- Karman+: first mission is "kilogram-scale" excavation at Apophis, launch mid-2028, nothing returned. Stated later: 1,000 to 5,000 kg of water from a 700 kg craft, and whole asteroids up to 100,000 kg (https://www.karmanplus.com/faq/).
- TransAstra: a 10 m capture bag closed on the ground in November 2026 and a 1 m bag in the space station in 2025; stated goal a 100 t asteroid, rendezvous 2028 or 2029.
- Planetary Resources and Deep Space Industries flew only Earth-orbit test hardware.
Every commercial date for an asteroid mission has slipped one to two years or lapsed.

PHYSICS. With storable chemical propulsion 20% of starting mass survives 5 km/s. Worked case: of 1,000 kg in low Earth orbit, 262 kg reaches the asteroid; a 150 kg spacecraft can then send about 240 kg home, a 200 kg one about 14 kg. Mass returned per mass launched:
- flown: 0.00006 (OSIRIS-REx);
- NASA's cancelled Asteroid Redirect Mission: 1.35 (13,535 kg boulder to lunar orbit; cost cap $1.4 billion without launch; https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170010282/downloads/20170010282.pdf);
- Keck 2012 study: 28 (a 500 t asteroid to lunar orbit, 18 t in orbit, $2.6 billion, 6 to 10 years; https://kiss.caltech.edu/final_reports/Asteroid_final_report.pdf);
- Sonter's aim: over 100.
Every ratio above one needs raw rock left in lunar orbit, a target with almost no return velocity, or propellant made at the asteroid. None is demonstrated.

WHAT WOULD HAVE TO CHANGE. Launch averaged $3,868 per kg to low Earth orbit in 2025 (https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/space-cargo-costs-set-to-plummet); Falcon 9 lists at $74 million. Ship-to-ship refuelling of Starship has slipped from March 2025 to 2027 and is not yet attempted. Holding on to a fast rotator, enclosing a tumbling rock, kilogram-scale excavation and processing at an asteroid have never been done; the one in-space test on meteorite material is a microbe-leaching experiment on the station.

GAPS. Largest sample to one tonne: four orders of magnitude. Demonstrated cost per gram to the platinum price: five. To propellant at $3 per gram: six.

NOT VERIFIED: any Tianwen-2 news after 13 October; SpaceX prices (third-party pages); several items seen only as search summaries (listed in the report's section 9). The plan's "about 120 grams" should read 121.6 g for the largest return and about 127 g in all.

### Recommended design

State the last rung with four fixed words and three amounts.

Words: Done (a spacecraft has done it at a body of this size and spin class), Under way (a spacecraft is in flight to do it), Planned (funded mission with a launch slot; print the stated date and when it was stated), Proposed (a paper, study or company statement).

Amounts: grams (a sample), kilograms (a demonstration), tonnes (mining or capture). The answers differ, so one word for the whole rung would mislead.

The rung for one asteroid is set only by its size and spin (measured or prior) against a fixed, dated record. Suggested classes:
- Over about 300 m and turning once in 4 h or slower: grams Done (three times); kilograms Planned (Karman+ at Apophis, no return); tonnes Proposed.
- 30 to 300 m, slow: grams not done at this size but the method is done; kilograms and tonnes Proposed.
- Under 30 m, or faster than about 2 h: grams Under way (Tianwen-2); the rest Proposed, whole-body capture only under about 10 m.
- Faster than about 10 minutes: never attempted.
- Spin unknown: say so, and print the prior.
Label these thresholds as judgement calls from three sampled asteroids. Put a 'last checked' date on the rung; Tianwen-2 may change the third class within months.

Per-asteroid sentences:
- Large and slow: 'Grams have been returned from three asteroids of this kind, at roughly $0.15 to 1 billion a mission. Nothing larger than a sample has been brought back from any asteroid.'
- Mid-sized and slow: 'No spacecraft has touched an asteroid this small; the sampling method that worked on three larger ones should apply if the spin is as slow as reported. Returning more than grams has only been proposed.'
- Small or fast: 'No spacecraft has touched an asteroid this small or this fast. One is trying now at an 18 m asteroid (Tianwen-2, result due 2027).'
- Add when spin is unmeasured: 'Its spin has not been measured, and that decides which of these applies.'

Site-wide statement: 'Three spacecraft have brought pieces of asteroids to Earth: about 127 grams in total, for about $1.4 billion. The largest haul, 121.6 grams from Bennu, cost about $8 million a gram and took seven years; platinum sold for about $42 a gram on 21 November 2026. All three asteroids were hundreds of metres wide and slow-turning; a typical reachable asteroid is about 15 m, most that small with a measured spin turn in minutes, and no spacecraft has touched one like it. One is trying now. No company has reached an asteroid. Bringing back tonnes has been designed on paper since 2012 and never built: it is four orders of magnitude beyond the largest sample in mass, and five to six in cost per kilogram.'

Show on the same page, with sources: the table of the three returns (grams, cost, years), the table of stated against achieved for each company, and the mass-returned-per-mass-launched table (flown 0.00006; cancelled NASA design 1.35; Keck paper 28; company statements 1.4 to 14), with the destination of each (Earth, lunar orbit, GEO) in its own column.

### Worked examples

- A typical 15 m object, spin unmeasured. (1) Found again? From its orbit record (measured). (2) Reached? Round trip of N km/s on the best date; one spacecraft has ever stayed at an asteroid under 100 m, and it got there in July 2026. (3) Big and slow enough to work on? About 15 m from brightness alone, likely 5 to 30 m, so about 130 to 28,000 tonnes of rock, most likely near 3,500. Gravity under a millionth of Earth's; a 1 cm/s push leaves for good. Spin not measured: most asteroids this small with a measured spin turn in minutes, and at ten minutes the surface at the equator is flung outward 200 times harder than gravity holds it (prior). (4) Worth anything? See the price range. (5) Could any of it be brought back? Grams: under way, not done. The three samples returned so far came from asteroids 20 to 60 times wider that turn in hours; a Chinese spacecraft is attempting it now at an 18 m asteroid that turns every 28 minutes. Kilograms: proposed. Tonnes: proposed. At about 3,500 tonnes the whole object is nearly three times the largest mass any capture study has designed for (1,300 tonnes).
- A 60 m slow rotator (for example 2011 AA37, about 65 m, 10-hour period graded unreliable). (1) Found again? Yes (measured). (2) Reached? 5.85 km/s; no spacecraft has stayed at an asteroid this small other than Tianwen-2 at its 18 m target. (3) Big and slow enough? About 230,000 tonnes if ordinary rock. At a 10-hour spin gravity beats the outward pull nearly twenty to one, so loose material can lie on it; escape speed about 3 cm/s. The spin is one uncertain measurement (measured, low confidence). (4) Worth anything? See the price range. (5) Could any of it be brought back? Grams: not done at this size; the method is done. Touch-and-go sampling has worked three times on asteroids 5 to 15 times wider, each mission costing about $0.15 to 1 billion and six to seven years from launch for at most 122 grams. Kilograms: proposed (one company named this object as a reference target in 2023; its first flight now goes elsewhere and returns nothing). Tonnes: proposed. NASA designed a mission to lift a 13.5-tonne boulder off a larger asteroid and cancelled it in 2017.
- A 6 m object a capture mission might bag. (1) Found again? Often not: many this faint were tracked for days (measured). (2) Reached? Among the cheapest, around 4 km/s. (3) Small enough to take whole? About 230 tonnes if rock; from brightness alone the mass is uncertain by a factor of about 20. The 2012 Keck design could move 250 to 1,300 tonnes. If it turns every two minutes its surface moves at 16 cm/s; stopping that takes under 10 kg of propellant, but nothing has ever enclosed a tumbling rock (prior). (4) Worth anything? See the price range. (5) Could any of it be brought back? Grams: under way, at an 18 m asteroid. The whole object: proposed. No spacecraft has captured, bagged or towed an asteroid; the only one ever moved was a 160 m moonlet nudged by a deliberate crash in 2022. A 10 m bag was inflated and closed on the ground in November 2026 and a 1 m bag inside the space station in 2025. The one costed design needed 18 tonnes in orbit, six to ten years and $2.6 billion to park a rock like this in lunar orbit, not on Earth. NASA dropped the whole-asteroid version in 2015 and the programme in 2017.

### Ways this misleads

- Cost per gram is not the cost of mining. It divides a science mission's whole budget (instruments, one to two years of mapping) by a small sample. It is the only demonstrated number, so it should read as 'what it has cost so far', not as a floor or a forecast.
- Destinations differ. The samples came to Earth's surface; Keck, ARM and TransAstra deliver to lunar orbit; Karman+ to geostationary orbit. A ratio of 28 to lunar orbit is not 28 to a buyer.
- Raw rock is not product. 500 tonnes of asteroid is not 500 tonnes of water or metal; one kilogram of platinum-group metal at the median iron-meteorite grade means processing about 25 tonnes of pure metal.
- Every published mass ratio above one depends on something undemonstrated: propellant made at the asteroid, a target with almost no return velocity, or leaving unprocessed rock in lunar orbit.
- The rocket-equation examples are sensitive. A 50 kg change in spacecraft mass moves the answer from about 240 kg to 14 kg, and the split between outbound and return legs is my assumption; JPL publishes only the total, and launchers supply part of it.
- The size and spin classes rest on three sampled asteroids. They are judgement calls, not physical limits, and Tianwen-2 may redraw them within months either way.
- Planned dates decay. Every commercial asteroid date read today has slipped by one to two years or lapsed; a plan should be printed with the date it was stated.
- Commercial failure so far says little about asteroids. Both AstroForge losses were radio and power faults near Earth. The asteroid-specific steps are untested commercially, which is not the same as failed, and not the same as feasible.
- Soft inputs: Hayabusa's sample mass was never recorded; Hayabusa2's cost is a 2012 plan figure; Hayabusa's cost is a quoted remark; OSIRIS-REx's is not inflation-adjusted; Chinese and Soviet costs are unpublished; yen conversions depend on the year chosen.
- Company cost and mass figures are statements, not results. Karman+'s '10,000x reduction in cost per kilogram' applied to OSIRIS-REx would still be about $840,000 per kg.
- This dates quickly. My newest Tianwen-2 status is 13 October 2026; a contact attempt since then cannot be ruled out. The rung needs a 'last checked' date.
- Launch price is the most visible trend and the least binding constraint here: launch is a small share of a $10 million to $1 billion mission, and falling launch prices do not change the return-leg mass budget.

## Research: value-inputs.md

**Headline.** A typical reachable asteroid (H 26.2, about 15 m) holds roughly $0.4–14 million of platinum-group metals in the rock at 20 Nov 2026 prices, with about 2 chances in 5 of none in separable form and 9 in 10 of no water; the tag depends on brightness alone until someone observes the object, and nothing of it can be brought back with technology that has flown.

Report written to docs/research/value-inputs.md. Tags: P = primary source read today, S = secondary read today, R = recalled.

WHAT IS THERE. Iron meteorites carry 14.7 / 40.8 / 107.9 ppm total platinum-group metals (PGMs) at the 10th/50th/90th percentile, range about 6–230, for 100% metal; the authors call these upper values, say pure-metal asteroids may not exist and that grade cannot be sensed remotely (Cannon et al. 2023, https://www.mining.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Precious-and-structural-metals-on-asteroids-.pdf, P). Every chondritic rock, stony or dark, carries 2–5.5 ppm: H 5.2–5.6, L 3.0–4.4, LL 1.9–2.3, CI 2.9–3.1, CM 3.7–4.0 (Horan et al. 2003, https://repository.geologyscience.ru/bitstream/handle/123456789/33788/Hora_03.pdf?sequence=1, P; rhodium added at a recalled ratio). In ordinary chondrites it sits in metal grains of about 18/9/4.5 wt% (my conversion of 8.4/4.1/2 vol%, https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.08734, P) at 29–52 ppm. Water: Ryugu 6.84 ± 0.34 wt%, Ivuna 12.73 ± 0.63, Bennu 0.84–0.95 wt% H (P); CM mean 7.0 (S). Chance that an object known only by brightness is hydrated: 3–17% (Rivkin & DeMeo 2019, https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02285, P). X-types split about 15% E / 31% M / 53% P (https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15872, P); 38% of M-class bodies are radar-consistent with metal (Shepard 2015, P abstract); chained, about 2.8% of unmeasured objects are metal-rich.

PRICES, 20 Nov 2026, Umicore (https://pmm.umicore.com/en/prices/platinum/, P): Pt $1,336/oz, Pd $912, Rh $8,950, Ir $10,200, Ru $1,900; osmium has no benchmark. Nickel $15,371/t, cobalt $33,525/t (Trading Economics, S). Platinum peaked near $2,923 on 26 Jan 2026 (S); iridium and ruthenium are 2.3 and 2.8 times their 2025 averages (USGS, https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-platinum-group.pdf, P). A meteoritic PGM mix is $87,945/kg now against $49,590/kg at 2025 averages (my calculation). World mine supply: Pt 178 t, Pd 197 t, Rh 22 t, Ir 7 t, Ru 30 t, osmium hundreds of kg (https://ipa-news.com/assets/pdfs/factsheets/2026-05-ipa-iridium-factsheet.pdf, P), $23.9 B a year in all. Water: ULA 2016 $3,000/kg low orbit, $1,000 high orbit, $500 lunar surface (P); Elvis 2014 $5,000; Hein 2020 $20,000 (assumed); Colvin 2020 cost $2,000–3,000/kg and demand 6,000–10,000 t over 2030–2050 (P abstract). Ceiling: Falcon 9 at $74 M is about $3,250/kg; Starship target $100–200/kg (S).

RESULT (my model, every assumption in report section 5.2). Chondritic rock holds $166–484 of PGMs per tonne; a 15 m, 4,400 t stony body $0.7–2.1 M. For H 26.2 with nothing measured: metal, 43% none, otherwise $0.36–14 M in the rock; water, 89% none, otherwise 330–11,000 t (16–1,700 t if size is not tied to class). DESIGN. Three rows (metal at dated prices; water in tonnes with capped dollars; what has flown), the chance of nothing first, then the 5th–95th percentile on one fixed log axis, no central value anywhere.

### Recommended design

THREE NAMED ROWS, always shown together, in this order.

Row 1, "Metal, at today's prices". Counts only the five PGMs with a market (Pt, Pd, Rh, Ir, Ru) that sit in iron-nickel metal grains, the form a simple process could separate; PGMs spread through dark clay-rich rock, bright enstatite rock or basalt count as nothing. Priced at one dated dealer snapshot per method release (here Umicore, 20 Nov 2026), date printed. No digging, refining, transport or price effect, and the page says so. Each metal is priced only up to 10% of a year's world mine supply (about $2.4 B in all); beyond that print tonnage and "more than the market could absorb". Osmium, nickel and cobalt appear as mass only.

Row 2, "Water, at a hypothetical orbit price". Tonnes of mineral-bound water are the primary figure. Dollars at three named prices ($100/kg Starship target, $1,000/kg ULA 2016 high orbit, $3,000/kg ULA 2016 low orbit and roughly today's Falcon 9 ceiling), computed for at most 2,000 t (five years of the only published demand forecast, 300-500 t a year from 2030). Print "Nobody buys water in orbit today."

Row 3, "Brought back, with what has flown". Not hypothetical: nothing. The most ever returned is 121.6 g; show what 121.6 g of the object's likeliest material would fetch (cents to about $26).

HOW TO EXPRESS IT. Chance of nothing first, as a frequency ("2 in 5"), with "nothing" defined per row. Then the 5th-95th percentile given that there is something ("nine cases in ten"), the 25th-75th as the darker part of a bar. One fixed log-dollar axis for every asteroid and row, $1 to $1 trillion, with three sourced marks: about $10 M (cheapest prospecting probe as stated by its builder), about $1 B (the mission that returned 121.6 g), $24 B (a year of world PGM supply). One significant figure, in words. Kilograms and tonnes beside the dollars. One line of price sensitivity ("at 2025 average prices: 44% less").

SO IT CANNOT BE QUOTED AS A POINT VALUE. Publish no median, mean or expected value anywhere, including the CSV and API (the mean is the dangerous one: 3% of cases carry two thirds of it). No field named value; only p_nothing, q05, q25, q75, q95, scenario_id, price_date, method_version. Scenario name, price date and "in the rock" sit inside the same sentence as the figures. No sorting or ranking by dollars. Row 3 never behind a click. The method page quotes JORC clause 51 and says why a range is printed anyway.

IN THE ROCK VERSUS RECOVERABLE. Separate, in three levels: in the rock; the most a perfect process could separate (which is how Rows 1 and 2 are defined, so they are upper bounds); recovered by anyone, ever (Row 3). Do not add a "recoverable at x% efficiency" line: any x is invented and would be the number quoted.

RESPONSE TO AN OBSERVATION. One joint model, redrawn: class, reflectivity, size and density are drawn together, an observation replaces a prior, and every row is recomputed, with the old range shown behind the new. For H 26.2: brightness only gives metal 43% none, $0.36-14 M, water 89% none; a rotation period changes nothing in the rock (it moves the workability rung); an S-type spectrum gives under 1 in 10 none, $0.3-5.7 M, no water; a C-type empties the metal row and makes water a coin flip; an X-type with no reflectivity widens the range ($1.1-190 M, 68% none), and the page should say a range can widen; S-type with a size good to 10% gives $0.5-3 M, a factor of 6, which is the floor from Earth and should be printed; a density ceiling near 5,000 kg/m3 halves the 3% metal-rich share and trims the top by 30-40%. Leverage runs size, class, reflectivity within X, density.

OPEN CHOICES FOR THE OWNER: metal row as PGMs in metal grains (43% nothing) or all PGMs (17% nothing); whether water shows dollars at all; the 10% market cap, the 2,000 t water cap and the three axis marks.

### Ways this misleads

- The top of any range will be quoted alone ('up to $14 million'). The design reduces this and does not prevent it.
- 'In the rock' is not value. The mining industry's JORC code forbids in-ground dollar figures for that reason; the page prints one only to show how small and wide it is, and must say so.
- The water row can out-inflate Asterank: uncapped at $1,000/kg any 30 m dark rock is 'worth $2 billion' and Ryugu '$31 trillion'. The price is hypothetical, no buyer exists, and the caps are my choices.
- Today's prices are a moving input: the PGM mix is 77% above its 2025 average while platinum is less than half its January 2026 record, and iridium, a 7-tonne-a-year market, supplies half the total.
- Meteorite grades are not asteroid grades: the atmosphere removes weak dark material and favours iron, and each stony group rests on two to four meteorites.
- Class shares come from brightness-selected surveys of larger objects; above H 25 they are extrapolation and surveys disagree by 10 points or more.
- Almost all the upside sits in the X-type branch, the least known: the 38% metal-dominated share comes from 29 main-belt bodies tens of km across.
- Sizes from brightness were 2 to 3 times too large for both small mission targets later measured; a factor of 2.5 in size is a factor of 16 in every figure.
- My reflectivities, their spreads and the flat density ranges are placeholders. Tying size to class or not changes the water tonnage twelvefold (330-11,000 t against 16-1,700 t).
- The definition of 'nothing' is a choice: counting PGMs in dark rock as nothing gives 43% for metal; counting all PGMs gives 17%.
- A mean or expected value would be dominated by the 3% of metal-rich cases (two thirds of the metal mean) and, for water, would be about $380 million for an object with no water nine times in ten.
- Several inputs were not read at source today: launch prices, nickel and cobalt spot prices, the Psyche figure's origin, NI 43-101, meteorite pore space, the direct asteroid densities, and the recalled element ratios used for rhodium and the iron-meteorite mix.
- Grade and density, and class and size beyond the reflectivity link, are treated as independent; they are probably not.
- Asterank's 125-fold mass factor is a fact about its code; whether it was intended is unknown, and the site should not claim more than that.

### Arithmetic errors

- Irons in meteorite falls: report says 'about 2% (49 of 1,102)'. 49/1,102 = 4.4% (source table: 4.6% of 1,062 classified).
- Example 2: 'under 1 in 10, if the class is wrong' is argued from 'at least 3 of the 24' disagreeing. 3/24 = 12.5%, which is more than 1 in 10.
- Nickel and cobalt 'worth less per kilogram than 1% of the cost of putting a kilogram into orbit': cobalt at $33.5/kg exceeds 1% of $3,250/kg, and both exceed 1% of $1,520/kg.
- Launch ceiling: $74M / 22.8 t = $3,246/kg and $97M / 63.8 t = $1,520/kg divide recovered-booster list prices by expendable capacities. The report flags this for Falcon Heavy but not Falcon 9, then builds 'about $1,500-3,300/kg' from both. With about 17.5 t (recalled) Falcon 9 is about $4,200/kg.
- H-chondrite range: the stated rule is 'lowest and highest meteorite in each group', but only 3 of the 9 H chondrites in Horan Table 1 were used. Stated 5.2-5.6 ppm ($459-484/t); table gives 3.3-6.3 ppm ($293-582/t).
- X split at fixed brightness printed as 56/33/11; the stated inputs (15/31/53 weighted by reflectivity^1.3 at 0.45/0.15/0.05) give 59/29/12.
- Platinum record printed as $2,924 in section 0 and $2,923 in section 2.1; source says $2,923.
- '300 to 500 t a year' is tagged [P abstract] for Colvin et al.; the abstract gives only 6,000-10,000 t over 2030-2050, and the tonnes are propellant, not water.
- Water row units: tonnes of water bound in rock at the asteroid are multiplied by prices for refined propellant delivered to LEO or L1. By Kutter's own ratios that overstates by 1.5x before transport and about 7.5x after (1,575 t of water mined per 210 t of propellant in LEO).
- 'Spectrum narrows the range from a factor of 40 to a factor of 18' depends on the placeholder size spread (10-23 m). Under the plan's size rule (7.5-30 m) an S-type spans $0.14M-$11M, a factor of about 80.
- Year-dollars mixed: water prices in 2000, 2011, 2016, 2019, 2022 and 2023 dollars are compared directly and the 2016 ULA figures are reused as 2026 price points; $3,000 in 2016 is about $4,200 in 2026 dollars.
- No error found in: troy-ounce conversions, market dollar sizes, sphere volumes and masses, the hand arithmetic of all three examples, the 60 m iridium shares (31% and 82%), the Ryugu '$31 trillion' illustration, or any Monte Carlo quantile.

### Design objections

- The 'nine cases in ten' range is conditional on there being something, so it covers 51% of all cases, not 90%. Readers will add '2 in 5 none' and '9 in 10' wrongly. Use one denominator: of 100 such asteroids, 43 none, 52 between $0.4M and $14M, 3 less, 3 more.
- The upper end ($14M) is the least stable number and the one that will be quoted. 2.9% of all cases exceed it and 84% of those are metal-rich; it sits on the join between two unlike populations. Changing the metal-rich share of M-types from 38% to 10% or 60% moves it to $8M or $35M while nothing else moves. Show two bars: the ordinary case (about 55 in 100, $0.3-6M) and the metal-rich case (about 3 in 100, $9-330M, not identifiable from Earth).
- Publishing no central value makes the top the headline. A one-sided statement cannot be turned into 'worth $X': 3 in 4 hold less than $2M, 19 in 20 less than $8M, 1 in 100 more than $80M (zeros counted).
- 'None ... in the rock' is false for dark asteroids: CI and CM carry 2.9-4.0 ppm, more than LL chondrites. The row applies a recoverability test while labelled 'in the rock'. With all PGMs counted the chance of none is 17%, not 43%. Name the row 'in metal grains' or count everything.
- The page prints, with a dollar sign beside each asteroid, the figure JORC clause 51 prohibits. Lead with kilograms and tonnes; give dollars as a second line labelled 'metal content at list prices', never 'worth' or 'value'.
- Cost marks on the same dollar axis invite subtraction: a range ending at $14M to the right of a '$10 million probe' mark reads as profit. That mark is a company's stated build cost for an unflown prospecting spacecraft, not a mining mission. Keep the $1 billion mark (a real mission that returned 121.6 g).
- A single day's dealer quote is too fragile to be 'the price': platinum fell 3.5% between the 10am quote and the close that day, 15% in nine days, 54% from January's record; iridium and ruthenium are at 2.3x and 2.8x their 2025 averages. Use a stated multi-year band or at least the monthly average, let price widen the range, and say that half the dollar figure is iridium, a 7-tonne-a-year market with no exchange price.
- The metal-rich branch is priced with CI element proportions that its own source (Cannon et al.) says fail for irons. Fix from the supplementary data before showing any metal-rich dollar figure, or show that branch in kilograms only.
- The water row should not carry dollars. Its tonnage moves 4x to 20x on the size and reflectivity model; its prices are for delivered propellant in 2016 dollars from a business case whose vehicle was dropped; its cap rests on a wrong 'only forecast' claim; and capped it still prints '$1-6 billion', which is only cap times price. Show tonnes beside physical comparisons (ISS received 3.3 t in a year; published scenarios run 59-500 t/yr of propellant).
- '$100/kg' as a named price point is a company's unmet target presented as a scenario. Label it 'not achieved' or drop it.
- Row 3 says what 121.6 g 'would fetch'. It would not fetch 44 cents; say 'contains 44 cents of platinum-group metal'. The $26 water figure puts a hypothetical price into the row described as not hypothetical. Add 'as of' and a date: a second asteroid sample is due in November 2027.
- A fixed axis from $1 to $1 trillion makes a factor-of-40 range look narrow (1.6 of 12 decades) and leaves nine empty decades pointing at 'trillion'.
- 'Almost certainly some' for an S-type reads as good news; it means mine-grade rock with metal as 4-18% of the stone. Colour classes also shift at small sizes (shock darkening can move S to X or C), so the wrong-class chance needs its own tested number, not 'under 1 in 10'.
- Drop the rule that a fast spin raises density: it has no source and the report's own table contradicts it.
- The 10% market cap is arbitrary. A published price-deflation rule exists (Kargel 1994, used by Sanchez et al. 2021). Whichever is used, the page must say it is a choice.
- Whole-number frequencies overstate what is known: '2 in 5' rests on class shares that surveys put 10 points apart, and the model holds the class mix fixed while S+Q falls from 50% at H 22 to 35% at H 28. Print the chance as a band until the project's own prior is tested.
- The printed ranges omit the three largest model uncertainties (metal-rich share, dark reflectivity, size rule). Either fold them in or print them beside the tag as 'this range would move by a factor of N if ...'.
