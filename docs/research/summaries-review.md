## Reviewer 1

**Verdict.** The direction is sound and the plan follows the verification round over round one on every contested point I checked, but it is not ready for the owner: he cannot make decision 1 because the plan gives no cost, no named user and no recommendation, and the opening never tells him what is asked of him. The single most important change is to rewrite the opening and section 5 so the outcome, the ask, the cost and a recommendation per decision are stated, including the choices the plan currently makes for him (going public before the work is done, dropping any value statement, journal publication, Python plus GPL). After that, fix three places where section 1 is more certain than the evidence: the "largest source of information" headline, the orbit-test verdict, and the named survivor 2011 AA37, whose period its own source grades as unreliable.

### Strongest points

- Publishing upper limits and 'no information' entries instead of detections only removes the selection that makes every existing drift list unusable for population statements.
- Keeping formal error bars and refusing reduced-chi-square rescaling is the right call on a contested point, and it is backed by measurement: rescaling gave up to 46% false alarms and missed the long-arc value in 3 of 13 truncated fits.
- The single-observation rule answers a real case: 2022 RD2 falls from 5.1 to 1.7 sigma when one position is removed.
- The drift-density result is stated as a range (3 to 27) with 'about half are better read as anomalies', which matches verification exactly rather than the first round's point value of 11.
- Barring circular values as truth is necessary: 10 of the 15 densities on file for reachable asteroids rest on the channels under test.
- Log loss with injected signals is the right design for a null result, and the plan states its own limits (160 labels fainter than H 25; 94% of the catalogue is prior). The gold-set power figure reproduces (0.77 at n = 12).

## Reviewer 6

**Verdict.** The plan is sound in outline and honest about limits, and its counts reproduce from the snapshots. A Planetary Science Journal referee would still reject four things as written: the "orbit fails for reachable asteroids" verdict, "pre-registered" tests on data already examined, "rules out iron" from brightness-based sizes at an undefined density, and pass bars that are already passed or cannot fail. The single most important change is to rescope the section 1 orbit verdict: it was tested at H near 20, not at H 26, and the plan's own evidence says the orbit matters most for the cheapest third of targets (lunar or artificial origin).

### Missing

- A plain statement of selection bias: the 154 drift detections are the light, pole-aligned, low-thermal-inertia objects by construction, so nothing about the population follows from them (nongrav.md section 2.4, "what a detection-biased sample should look like").
- The size-free consistency test for small objects with both force terms: drift divided by radiation pressure must stay under about 0.09. It needs no size or albedo, passes 2020 GE (0.011) and fails 2006 RH120 (0.33). It is in nongrav.md section 3.1 and not in the plan.
- A join of the plan's own channels on the nine sieve survivors under 6 km/s: four have a JPL drift (2011 AA37 at 12.5 sigma, 2001 CQ36 at 17, 613862 at 8) and three have measured sizes. These are where the density bound applies inside the target class.
- The second fitting engine and the comparison with ESA's solutions as explicit WP3 steps; section 2.6 already relies on the first.
- An error model for close approachers: timing errors and trailed images at 21-71 arcsec a minute, and the absence of per-observation uncertainties in the public record (0 of 1,559 for 2011 MD).
- Written rules for tumblers (Toutatis, Apophis) and for objects temporarily captured by Earth (2020 CD3, 2006 RH120).
- A statement that "reachable" means JPL's human round-trip list (450 days or less, fixed launch window) and that capture or one-way missions rank objects differently, for example by return speed. WP4 names three mission types but the sieve uses one delta-v.
- The strength and spin literature behind any spin gate, and Pravec et al. 2012 on systematic H offsets, neither of which is in docs/research/ (recalled, not re-fetched).

### Strongest points

- Section 2.4 is physically right: the method sees dense objects worst (bare iron spinning fast shows almost no drift), an unexplained acceleration is not ice, and no per-object composition is claimed.
- Radiation pressure is reported as area-to-mass with density only under a stated size, and values derived from the same signals are barred as truth (10 of 15 catalogued densities and 11 of 18 thermal inertias on the list are circular).
- The error-model choices follow the verification: keep formal covariances, never rescale by chi-square (up to 46% false alarms), inject into real residuals, publish limits and nulls with measured sensitivity.
- The gold set is used as a final exam only, and the power figure checks: 12 objects catch "claims 90%, delivers 60%" 77.5% of the time at a 2.6% false-positive rate.
- 2022 RD2 is described accurately and with the right caution, and the case studies are framed as tests that may fail.
- The headline counts reproduce from today's snapshots: 286 and 726; the elimination table rows; 83.0% with arcs of 90 days or less; 118 / 27 / 15 / 11 / 7 / 3 for the density bound; 573 drift and 12 radial-term detections.
- Putting size and spin ahead of composition is the right order: mass goes as diameter cubed and the size is the worst-known quantity.

## Concept: Ink and Outline: a field guide to the reachable asteroids

The visitor meets one sentence and a sheet of 7,229 small marks, one per reachable asteroid, almost all of them pale; the finding is the picture before anything is clicked. Each mark opens an entry with the same six rows for every object. A measurement is inked in with its source; anything unmeasured is an outline as wide as the ignorance, holding the measured values of the most similar objects and a tab saying which observation would fill it and when that is possible. They stay because the blanks are specific (this one opens in February, that one cannot be pointed at until a survey finds it again), and they return because the sheet changes: about twelve new blank entries a week, an occasional outline turning to ink with the old guess kept behind it and scored, and a few sealed blanks with dates on them between 2027 and 2029.

### Signature interactions

- **Contact sheet**: Sees every entry at once as a four-cell stamp, in five bands by round-trip cost, brightest first within a band. Ink sits at the start of each band and thins to nothing. The cheapest band starts already faint, because nothing under 5 km/s is brighter than H 24.

Three arrangements, each a 400 ms slide of the same marks:
- By cost (default).
- By brightness: one run with size ticks in the gutter (about 140 m, 55 m, 20 m, 10 m). Ink is dense at the top and gone by about H 25.
- By date found: one band per year. Recent years are large and blank, and the last row carries this week's ticks.

The pointer brings up a loupe: the neighbourhood at 9x with the centre entry's designation and one-sentence summary. Click opens the entry. On a phone it is a picture with five band links. Data: Per object: designation, minimum round-trip delta-v, H, first-observation date, one state byte (2 bits each for size, spin, surface, sunlight push: outline, limit, measured, not yet examined), a changed-this-release flag, and the generated one-sentence summary. Band edges and the size-for-H ticks from the pre-registration. About 30 bytes an object plus the sentence. Cost: About 180 lines of JS: three layouts, three batched fill passes per frame, position interpolation, arithmetic hit-test, loupe. About 40 lines in the generator for the PNG fallback and image map. Low risk; 7,229 marks need no GPU. Fails if: The ordering spreads ink evenly and it reads as grey static, which is why the default is banded and brightness-sorted, not a plain sort by cost. Readers take position on the sheet for position in the sky. The dynamics release tones the fourth cell on about 17% of stamps at once and the sheet looks better informed than it is; limits must stay visibly lighter than measurements. It is not interactive on a phone.
- **The blank: outline, neighbours, tab**: On an entry, an unmeasured row is never empty space. Every entry uses the same axes: size 1 m to 1 km (log); spin 10 s to 100 h (log) with lines at 10 minutes and 2.2 hours; surface as a four-part bar; sunlight push as density times diameter with bands for rubble, rock and iron.

An unmeasured row draws four things on its axis:
- An outline spanning the population range for an object this bright.
- Inside it, small ink dots for the measured values of the most similar entries, each a link, with the count in words: "of 61 measured objects this faint, 59 turn faster than 2.2 hours".
- A tab on the outline's right edge saying what would fill it and when: "one night's lightcurve, 2 m class; window Feb to Jul 2027, V 17.5", or "cannot be pointed at until a survey finds it again", or "radar or thermal infrared; cannot be requested".
- One line in words.

Hover or tap a dot for the neighbour's name and value. For sunlight push the blank reads "the tracking carries no information", and the row draws the floor: what these nights of positions could have detected. Data: Per object: H and its error; measured values with source ids and any conflicts; nights and positions tracked; next window (start, end, peak magnitude, pointable or survey-only flag); the dynamics value or upper limit and its sensitivity floor. Global: prior tables by H bin (size range, spin fractions with both denominators, class base rates with the between-survey spread) and the measured sample behind each bin (about 197 sizes, 416 periods, 302 classes on the list). Cost: Static: the generator writes each row as inline SVG, with the neighbour strip built once per H bin and referenced by entries, so no JS is needed to read it. About 300 lines of generator templates. About 60 lines of JS for dot hover and for the tab state ("open now") computed from today's date. Risk: the row drawing gets written twice, in Python and in JS; keep JS out of drawing rows. Fails if: The neighbours are a biased sample (measured objects are the bright close-passers, and one infrared survey's dark fraction is a selection effect), so the dots make the guess look better founded than it is. Faint bins have under five neighbours; true, but the outline is bare. Every entry in an H bin has identical outlines, so thousands of entries differ only in the orbit row and the tab.
- **Pencil in**: Thresholds on the axis cut an outline into outcomes, each with its odds from the prior and its consequence. Tapping an outcome pencils it in:
- The row shows a dashed mark in the pencil colour.
- The three mission lines under the title (bag a whole small one, work on the surface of a larger one, fly past) and the summary sentence re-evaluate in pencil.
- A banner reads "Pencilled in. Not a measurement." with Erase.

The entry then states what that one observation would settle, for example: "A lightcurve: about 1 in 12 it shows a period over 2.2 hours and this becomes the second standing entry under 6 km/s; otherwise it is out for surface work."

Where no outcome changes anything, the outline has no cuts and the row says: "Nothing one observation could show would change where this stands." Without scripts the same outcomes are a three-line table in the row. Data: The gate thresholds and mission definitions from WP4, frozen in the pre-registration. Per object: chain results (out, standing or untested for each mission) and, for each untested gate, the prior odds of each outcome. Window fields. All arithmetic in the browser is threshold checks. Cost: About 120 lines of JS and no new drawing code; it toggles classes and rewrites three text nodes. The risk is in the science, not the code. Fails if: It ships before the mission gates are fixed and tested, and so dramatises thresholds nobody has justified. For the median entry (about 20 m: too big to bag, too small to work on) no observation changes anything, so it is live on perhaps 50 to 200 entries near a gate (my estimate). The odds are shown to a precision the calibration set cannot support; only about 160 labelled objects are fainter than H 25.
- **Three-lane key**: The plan's elimination table as the guide's identification key, on one static page. Each question is a row: reach under 5, 6 or 7 km/s; brighter than H 24; orbit good enough to find again; spin slower than 2.2 hours.

Each row has three lanes:
- Out: a measurement rules it out.
- Standing: a measurement passes it.
- Untested: not measured, with the number the prior expects to pass.

Under 6 km/s today that reads: 726; 10 bright enough; 9 findable; then for spin, 1 standing, 1 out, 7 untested. Every count links to that list of strips. The Untested lane joined to observing windows is the "worth observing" list; 21 entries under 6 km/s have a pointable window brighter than magnitude 22 before 2030. With scripts, pointing at a count lights those stamps on a small copy of the sheet. Data: Per object: gate results (derivable from the state byte, H, orbit condition code and period) and window fields. Thresholds from the pre-registration. Cost: A generated HTML table plus list pages. About 60 lines of JS for the highlight. Low. Fails if: Readers take the sketch gates (H 24, 2.2 hours) for mission requirements. The brightness gate hides the factor-of-two size error; the two small targets checked were 2 to 3 times smaller than assumed, so entries near the line need a "near the line" note. The counts rest on one database's period field until the union of period sources is in.
- **The record: ghost outlines, sealed blanks, flaps**: When a row is measured, the ink lands on the axis and the outline shown the week before stays behind it, lighter. Kamoʻoalewa is the model case: brightness gave 24 to 107 m, the measurement is 18 m, outside the outline.

One page, three parts:
- Blanks with a date: entries whose outline is sealed and hashed before a known result, each with its date tab. Kamoʻoalewa sample, late 2027; 2015 BM510 flyby, April 2028; Apophis, 2029; Dimorphos mass as a reference entry.
- Every outline inked since the first release, newest first, each as its row with ghost and ink and the word "inside" or "outside". A running tally: "N outlines inked; we drew 80% ranges; M landed inside."
- The dozen known-answer entries, drawn as the guide would have drawn them with the truth withheld. A native disclosure element lifts the flap to show the ink. Data: A diff of consecutive releases: object, row, the prior shown before, the value after, its source and publication date, and whether that source was already an input to the prior. Hashes and dates of the sealed predictions. The gold-set predictions, which exist only after the one-shot exam. Cost: About 100 lines in the generator for the diff and the page. No JS; the flap is a details element. Risk: upstream catalogues revising values create false fills and un-fills, so an event must be defined as a new source id, not a changed number. Fails if: Almost nothing gets measured: the estimate is 5 to 15 new spin periods a year without telescope access, so the list stays short and "M of N inside" means nothing below about 20 events. Class rows are scored against labels that disagree with each other 21 to 33% of the time. The known-answer flaps show the same base-rate bar a dozen times, because class has no per-object predictor; size and spin are the rows worth revealing.
- **Strips: the list is the comparison**: Every list in the guide (a cost band, a lane of the key, new this week, an entry's neighbours) is a table of strips. A strip is one 28 px line per entry: stamp, designation, reach, then the size, spin, surface and sunlight-push rows as 64 px miniatures on the same axes as the full entry, then the tab.

Because the axes line up, reading down a column is the comparison. Column headers sort. A pin mark on any strip or entry adds it to a compare list carried in the URL, which shows the pinned entries as lanes inside each full-size row. The link can be sent to someone. Data: The same compact data file; nothing extra. Cost: Strips are static HTML and SVG from the generator. About 80 lines of JS for sorting and about 100 for the compare page. The compare page is the one place JS would have to draw rows; defer it. Fails if: The entries compared are all blank: four identical outlines. On a phone the miniatures do not fit and the strip falls back to stamp, name and one sentence.
- **True-rate spin mark**: The entry's portrait is the object's size drawn to scale: nested outlines for the smallest and largest plausible size, or a solid disc when measured. If a period is measured, a tick on the rim turns at the real rate. 2022 OB5 goes round once every 92.5 seconds while you read; 2011 AA37 barely moves. With no period there is no tick, and the words "spin not measured". Data: Period in seconds with a quality or conflict flag; size range or measured size. Cost: No JS. The generator writes the period into a CSS animation duration, and the reduced-motion preference stops it. About 15 lines of CSS. Fails if: Only about 416 entries have a period. Periods with factor-of-two aliases or source conflicts (13 of about 204 shared periods disagree by more than 20%) make "real rate" false; those must get no animation. It can read as decoration if it is used anywhere but the entry.

**Visual direction.** The one idea: ink versus outline, held at three scales. A stamp (four cells, 5 to 56 px), a strip (one line) and a plate (the full entry) are the same object at different sizes. At every size:
- Solid means measured, with a source.
- Hatched means a limit only.
- Outline means a guess from the population.
- An empty cell with a dot means the guide has not examined it yet.

State is carried by fill style, never by hue, so it survives greyscale and colour blindness. At sheet scale, where 2 px cannot hold an outline, three tones stand in.

Colour: warm off-white ground and near-black ink. Two accents, one job each: red-orange for "changed since the last release" (ticks and rings), and a grey-blue dashed line for the visitor's pencil. The dark scheme inverts to chalk on slate. No gradients, shadows, rounded corners, photographs or star fields.

Type: system serif for sentences and the headline. System monospace with tabular figures for designations, numbers, axis labels and small capital captions. No web fonts.

Density: a reference book's. A plate shows all six rows on one laptop screen at 72 px a row. Lists are 28 px strips. Hairline rules, a 4 px grid, pixel-aligned squares.

Tone: flat declarative sentences. "Not measured." "Turns once every 92.5 seconds." "Cannot be pointed at until a survey finds it again." No "explore", no "treasure", no currency.

Feel: a large, quiet collection that is mostly unfilled. Every member has the same fixed sheet, a few are rare and complete, the rules are visible and can be poked, and the response comes inside one frame.

**What makes it alive.** Day to day, with no rebuild: tabs computed in the browser from stored window dates read "open now", "opens in 9 days" or "closed". A line on the front counts the entries observable tonight.

### Critique of draft section 4

- The home page is a control panel. Most visitors do not interact, and a table whose first column reads 286, 0, 0, 0, 0 does not state the finding. No sentence on the page says what was found.
- The sieve treats 'not measured' as 'failed'. The step 'with a measured rotation period' drops 7 of 9 objects under 6 km/s for lack of a measurement. That is the exact distinction the project exists to make, and the 7 are the most useful rows on the site.
- Sliders contradict the science plan's rule that thresholds are fixed before looking. A reader who drags H from 24 to 25 produces survivors the project has not vetted and would not stand behind.
- 'A measured value, or a prior drawn as a wide bar, or a blank' does not define when a row is a prior and when it is blank. Every unmeasured row has a prior from H, so the real states are measured, limit, prior, 'tracking carries no information' and 'not yet examined'. The draft gives no grammar for them.
- The plan's own risk section says the site has to make a mostly-prior catalogue interesting, and section 4 does not say how. A wide bar is identical for every object of the same brightness, so thousands of entry pages would be the same page.
- The evidence ledger assumes several channels combining into an answer. The findings left one base rate, no orbit signal and a coarse dynamics limit for a minority, so the ledger has nothing to show on most entries.
- Phase 1 promises 'known answers' and a sieve by mission type after step 1, but predictions against the gold set are a one-shot exam at step 3 and mission types are WP4. The site phasing does not follow the science gates.
- The known-answers page would show the same class prediction for every object, because the only class predictor left is a base rate. It also asks a non-astronomer to guess a class, about which they have no intuition to correct. Size and spin are where reality surprised (18 m against 24 to 107 m; 92 seconds), and they are guessable.
- 'What makes it move' is asserted but no surface shows change beyond a line above the sieve. The draft does not face the arithmetic: about 650 new blank entries a year against 5 to 60 new measurements, so the catalogue gets blanker over time.
- Eight page types for one maintainer. Three answer 'how far to trust this' (Known answers, Scoreboard, What we cannot know), two are the same filter (Sieve, Observe next), and Dynamics repeats entry content.
- The example figure for the article, dragging the length of a tracking arc, teaches what verification refuted: arc length does not predict information (a factor of 250 spread within one arc class). Nights, visits and a few late positions do.
- File count is checked for today only. HTML plus JSON per object is 14,458 files now and grows about 1,300 a year, reaching the free tier's 20,000 limit around 2031. The host also deletes deployments older than 30 days, so old releases cannot live there.
- The phone and the no-script reading are named as principles but not designed. Nothing says what a threshold table with 7,229 moving objects is on a 390 px screen.
- What is right and should stay: uncertainty as the headline, no dollar values, no orrery, static files, sources on every number, entries that keep their history, and the machine-readable twin.

### Risks

- Sameness. Thousands of plates differ only in the orbit row and the tab. A visitor who lands on one from a search sees a template. The design bets that the tab, the neighbours and the tracking history are enough, and that may be wrong.
- Ink reads as certainty and as merit. Measured values carry errors and conflicts, and a fully inked stamp is not a good target. The plate needs a 'sources disagree' mark, and the copy must say ink means known, not worthwhile.
- False blanks. One upstream database is stale (a sampled object had a published lightcurve it did not show). A guide that says 'not measured' about an object with a paper is a public error. Each plate needs a plain 'tell us what we missed' address, and the ledger must check every source before printing a blank.
- Outlines too narrow. Priors built from measured neighbours inherit their selection, and both small targets checked fell below their brightness-based size range. If the first fills land outside, the record shows it in public. That is the design working, but the project has to be ready for it.
- Long quiet stretches. Many weeks will have zero rows filled. Liveness then rests on new entries and window tabs, which is thin.
- Completeness as a score. A percentage invites reading measurement count as progress, and it will fall as discovery outruns follow-up. It must be framed as the state of the field, not the project's performance.
- Science dependencies. Mission lines, pencil-in and the key rest on WP4 gates that do not exist yet. Naming 2011 AA37 as the survivor rests on a single database field and must be re-checked first.
- The headline goes stale at step 2. Once about 17% of entries gain a tracking-based limit, 'nothing measured except brightness and position' needs rewording.
- Two drawing code paths. If JS starts drawing rows that the generator also draws, one person maintains the same picture twice. Only the sheet and the compare page should draw in the browser.
- Metaphor creep. 'Field guide' can suggest things identifiable on sight, or tip into costume. It should stay a structure (fixed entries, a key, an index) with no paper textures or mock natural-history styling.
- Licences. Showing neighbours' periods and classes per object leans on compilations with restrictive terms (one lightcurve database is all rights reserved; one survey's data are non-commercial). Cite the original papers and publish derived values with pointers.
- Access. The canvas sheet needs its text equivalent kept in step with the picture, and 2 px tone differences vanish on poor displays. The phone sheet is a picture, not a tool.

## Concept: By Elimination: one edition-dated explorable article, with the catalogue behind it as reference

The site is one page that argues a case and carries an edition date. It opens on every asteroid a spacecraft could reach, drawn as 7,229 dots: pale where only the orbit and brightness are known, inked where anything has been measured, with a box over the cheap-and-big corner that is almost empty. Six short sections follow, each headed by its claim and each with one figure on the real data that the reader can move: try to predict composition from the orbit and fail, delete the 13 observations that carried a whole measurement, shift the sieve and read off what one night at a telescope would settle. People return because the page changes under them: numbers are bound to the weekly catalogue, sentences that stop being true mark themselves, sections publish only when their science passes a stated test, and the forecasts a reader marked a guess against resolve on known dates from 2027 to 2029.

**Visual direction.** The one idea: ink is evidence. The ground is off-white and the ink near-black. Solid ink is used only for something measured, pencil grey (outline, hatch, pale band) for a prior, and bare paper for nothing. The page is therefore mostly paper, and it darkens only as measurements land. The same field of 7,229 dots returns as a small static strip at the head of every section, re-inked by that section's question. The strip above the orbit section has no ink in it at all, which is the null result as a picture.

Colour: monochrome plus one warm red-orange accent with exactly one meaning: this can be moved. Every grip is the same accent square. Nothing else is coloured. Dark and bright asteroids are filled and open dots, so no class palette is needed. The dark theme is the inverse with the same rules.

Type: system serif for prose at 18 to 19 px and about 66 characters a line; system monospace with tabular figures for every number, axis and label. No downloaded fonts. Words a person wrote are in serif; values the build generated are in monospace and underlined as source links, so the reader can see which is which.

Density: figures are dense and exact, with hairlines, labels placed on the marks, and no boxed legends. The page around them is sparse. Section headings are full-sentence claims. There are no cards, icons, gradients, glows, star backgrounds, parallax or scroll-driven animation, and the reader's scroll is never taken over. Controls answer in the same frame with no easing.

Tone: plain and slightly dry. It says "none", "not known" and "we were wrong about this" in the same voice as everything else. The feel aimed at is a quiet, wide, mostly empty map with a few named landmarks, tools that respond the instant they are touched, and a scoreboard that records the misses.

**What makes it alive.** The mechanism: the article is a hand-written HTML source with two kinds of binding, resolved by the weekly deterministic build with no model in the loop.
- Bound numbers. The pipeline's export step writes a facts file of about 150 named values, each with its source links, the script that produced it and last week's value. Every number in the prose is a placeholder filled from it and rendered as a link to its source. A number that changed carries a small margin note ("was 7,226").
- Guarded sentences. A sentence that could become false carries a condition, for example "nothing under 5 km/s is brighter than H 24". The build evaluates each one. A sentence whose condition fails is published struck through with "true until 28 Nov 2026" and a link to the object that broke it, until the author rewrites it. The half-dozen first-screen sentences have variants written in advance for zero, one and many.
- The figures read the same data file as the catalogue, so they are current by construction.

Week to week, a returning visitor sees:
- The edition line with a new date and a count of changes, and a change list at the foot in three kinds: numbers that moved and which object moved them, the dozen or so new arrivals as links, and any sentence that flagged itself.
- With scripts, the line is personal: "since you were here on 21 Nov: 41 found, 3 measured for the first time, 1 prediction scored", from a stored edition id and a small changes file.
- New arrivals marked in the field, and in "Four days" this week's discoveries with tonight's brightness.
- Two running counts under Figure 1: found this year and measured this year. About 650 are found a year and a few dozen are measured, so the paper grows faster than the ink, and the page shows that.

Year to year:
- Sections publish when their gate passes. The contents list is the gate table: the dynamics section and the forecasts each show the test they must pass until they do, and a failed test is published in that slot instead.
- The scoreboard resolves on known dates: Dimorphos in 2027, the Kamoʻoalewa sample in late 2027, the 2015 BM510 flyby in April 2028, Apophis in 2029, plus a continuous trickle as surveys publish periods and colours.
- The visitor's own marks from "Place your mark" are still in their browser when the truth arrives.
- The sieve's table changes whenever someone measures a rotation for an open candidate, and the corner in Figure 1 stops being empty the week a large, cheap object is found.

### Critique of draft section 4

- The home page has no sentence. "The table in section 1, live" shows a newcomer the counts 0, 10 and 46 against terms like H 24 and 2.2 hours that they do not yet know. Most visitors never touch a control, so the first screen states no finding in words.
- The sieve treats unmeasured as eliminated. The row "with a measured rotation period" takes 10 objects to 2, but the other 8 are not ruled out; the plan itself says one night would settle most of them. That contradicts 4.1, "unknown is shown as unknown". The sieve needs three states (in, out, open) and an expected count from priors.
- Uncertainty is called the headline, but the headline table is integer counts from hard cuts. "Brighter than H 24" is a cut on a size that is uncertain by a factor of two either way, and nothing in 4.2 says where that shows.
- The example figure for the article contradicts the verification. "Drag the length of a tracking arc" would teach that longer arcs carry more information. The refits found precision is not set by arc length (a 250-fold spread within one arc class); for 2011 MD, 1,543 positions in the first 12 days carried nothing and 13 positions on days 41 to 47 cut the error twelvefold.
- The most publishable result has no figure and no prominence. The orbit null, the subject of the planned Research Note, "lives" on page six, What we cannot know, and the only interactive example named anywhere is a dynamics one.
- The phasing launches without the part the draft says attention would come from. Phase 1 is a ledger site and the article is phase 2, so the front door has to be rebuilt once people start arriving.
- Phase 1 lists Known answers, but the science plan holds the gold set back as a final exam at step 3. Showing withheld-truth predictions after step 1 spends the exam early.
- The calibration curve sits under a twelve-object quiz. The plan says twelve objects can catch only gross overconfidence, and all but one are far larger than the targets, so the page would imply a validation at 20 metres that does not exist.
- Nothing protects the prose from the weekly rebuild. The draft says what changes (new objects, new measurements) but not how a sentence containing a number stays true when no person or model is in the loop, nor how a returning visitor sees what changed beyond a line above the sieve.
- The entry page is designed for the rich case. "A ledger shows what each piece of evidence did to the answer" describes perhaps 450 entries; the other 6,780 are near-identical pages of priors, and that case needs to be the designed one.
- Eight pages is a lot for one maintainer and splits one argument across five of them. The same explanation of why spin matters would be needed on the sieve, the entry, Observe next and the method page.
- The script budget is understated. "A few hundred lines" fits the ledger site; the research report puts each hand-made figure at 200 to 500 lines, so four to six figures is 1,000 lines or more.
- Per-object pages plus per-object JSON is about 14,500 files today and grows by about 1,300 a year, which reaches the free tier's 20,000-file limit in about four years.
- "Pick a mission type" as the first control asks the visitor for domain knowledge before the page has given them any.

### Risks

- Craft and correctness. About 1,300 lines of hand-written figure code for one maintainer, and a wrong figure is a wrong public claim. Mitigation: the generator writes every figure's resting state and caption, the script checks its own numbers against them on load, and it leaves the static figure in place on any mismatch.
- The first screen names one asteroid. 2011 AA37 rests on a single database period in a sketch the plan itself calls not a result; about 6% of periods held by two sources disagree by more than 20%. If it is wrong, the front door advertises the error, and industry readers may take a named survivor as a recommendation.
- The headline is a null and a nearly empty corner. Visitors who came for a ranked list of valuable asteroids will leave, and the plan already flags this as a weaker headline than a treasure map.
- Two of six sections depend on gates that may not pass for months, or may fail. The article will have visible holes, which is honest but can read as unfinished.
- Guarded sentences need a person. If the author is away, struck-through sentences accumulate on the public page. That is preferable to silent rot, but it is visible.
- Length on a phone. Most readers stop after the first screen, so sections 3 to 5 serve a minority; the cost of those figures has to be justified by reviewers and returning readers, not by traffic.
- Figure 3 invites p-hacking. A reader hunting for the best split will find small apparent gains unless the grey band is the null for the best split anywhere; if that band is computed wrongly, the figure teaches the opposite of the result.
- Position reads as knowledge. For about 97% of dots the vertical position is a prior from brightness, yet a dot on an axis looks measured. The band and the wording carry a lot of weight.
- The scoreboard can stay empty. Nothing dated resolves before 2027, and the continuous trickle depends on upstream databases that lag by months or years.
- Extra pipeline work that the plan does not list: daily ephemerides for new objects (sequential JPL requests), per-night refits for the case studies, a facts file that keeps last week's values, and a changes file.
- The static dots and the scripted overlay must share one scale, and the fixed-size-dot SVG technique needs testing across phone browsers before it is relied on.
- An article is more quotable than a table, so the bar for outside review is higher. No section should go public before the named human reviewer has read it, and the prose must be the author's own with AI use disclosed.
- Licences. Showing classes and periods derived from all-rights-reserved or non-commercial sources is safe only as derived values with pointers; Figure 3 ships binned counts for that reason, and the research framing has to hold.

## Concept: Wrong If

The visitor meets three plain claims about the 7,229 reachable asteroids, each printed with the condition that would prove it wrong and when that evidence is due: the orbit says nothing about the rock; nothing has been measured for 94% of them; under 6 km/s one object is left standing once size, orbit quality and spin are applied. Below the claims one time axis runs from 2026 to 2030 with a rule at today. Ahead of the rule are the mission results, survey releases and telescope windows that can settle a prediction; behind it is everything that has settled, including the misses and the windows that closed with nobody looking. Every object carries the same unit, a prediction drawn as a band with an empty slot where the truth will go, so a catalogue that is mostly priors and blanks reads as a book of open predictions and not as an empty table. People stay because it is the one place that says what would change its own mind and when to check back, and they return to see whether the rule has crossed something.

**Front door.** Numbers below are today's preliminary values; the generator prints whatever the release contains. The whole screen is HTML and inline SVG written at build time. It needs no script and no click.

### Signature interactions

- **The due line**: Sees the 2026 to 2030 axis with the rule at today. Tapping any bar or tick opens its slip in place below the line: the prediction in one sentence, the band, WRONG IF, what counts as truth and from which source, VOID IF (for example a flyby that passes outside 300 km), the date it was written with commit and DOI, and a short dated approach log of sourced milestones. Dragging a second grey rule leftward rewinds: slots and crosses appear as they stood at that issue. On a quiet day the line still says something true: which window is open now, which closes next, and how far the rule is from the nearest mission result. Data: A hand-kept file of at most 12 named items (id, statement, band or probability, written date, method version, hash, truth source, void condition, due start and end, date confidence: firm, window, year only or unscheduled; approach-log entries each with a source URL and a checked-on date). NHATS window fields (start, end, peak magnitude, pointable or survey-recovery flag) for the under-6 km/s set. A per-issue event list: settled, window opened, window closed with or without a measurement. Cost: About 120 lines of generator code for the SVG and the fallback list, about 120 lines of JavaScript (today rule, relative dates, open slip, rewind). Risk: label collisions near 2027, and hand-kept dates going stale; the generator flags any item not re-checked in 90 days. Fails if: The nearest dated item is more than six months off and no window is open, so the rule sits in white space. Half the mission bars are year-long hatches and read as wallpaper. A mission slips or fails and nobody updates the file. The named predictions are never written before their truth arrives, which turns the line into a mission calendar that agencies already do better.
- **The strike list**: On the book page every reachable asteroid under the chosen cap is one column of three cells: can it be found again, is it big enough, does it spin slowly enough. A cell is solid (measured, fails), white (measured, passes) or hatched at the density of the prior (nobody knows). Buttons set the cap to 5, 6 or 7 km/s or all; after gate 3, three mission buttons change the thresholds. Three counts update on each press: struck by measurement, undecided, standing. Pointing at a column names the object and links its entry. One toggle keeps only undecided objects that can be pointed at before 2030: about 21 columns, each a prediction one night would settle. On a phone the columns wrap into rows by delta-v band. This is the sieve from the draft, shown as open predictions instead of a filter. Data: Per object: delta-v, H, orbit condition code, window start, end, magnitude and pointable flag, a bitmask of what is measured, the number of independent sources for the period, and either P(big enough) and P(slow enough) per threshold set or the prior parameters to compute them from H. Cost: About 180 lines of JavaScript on Canvas 2D, plus a build-time SVG for the under-6 view and an HTML table for the 5, 6 and 7 km/s counts. About 12 quantised columns for 7,229 rows, 150 to 300 KB. Risk: touch picking on narrow columns. Fails if: Readers take hatched cells for candidates. A loose cap makes the standing list look like a list of recommended targets. At 5 km/s everything is struck or hatched at the first gate, which is the finding but leaves nothing to press. The one white column rests on a wrong database value: 13 of about 204 shared periods disagree between sources by more than 20%.
- **The record**: Every settled prediction, newest first, drawn as its band with the truth as an accent mark. Yes-or-no predictions use the same glyph as a split bar: the mark lands in the wide part or the thin part. Forward predictions are solid; hindcasts on known answers are hollow and in a separate block. Above the list is one row per kind of prediction: a grey range showing what an honest forecaster would score with this many settled, and a dot for what happened. With 3 settled the grey spans nearly the full width and the caption says it proves nothing. No percentage is printed before 30 settle. A miss is a mark outside the band in the same ink and size as a hit, labelled 'outside' with the factor ('truth 18 m, band 24 to 107 m'), followed by a dated note of at most 80 words written by the human author with one of three verdicts: within the expected miss rate, method changed (with the diff), or the terms were poor (noted, never voided afterwards). A line states expected outside against observed outside, so too few misses also counts against the catalogue. The last five settlements show on the front page whatever their result. A standing claim that falls stays on the front page, struck through, with the issue it fell in. Data: Settlement events: prediction id, band or probability, method version, truth value or outcome, truth tier (returned sample or spacecraft; direct measurement; model-dependent values cannot settle anything), source, date measured, date ingested, contested flag when sources disagree. After gate 3: sealed-set calibration bins and the exam results. Cost: About 100 lines of generator code (binomial ranges, SVG rows), at most 60 lines of JavaScript for filters. The bulk file needs only outcomes and pointers, not upstream values, which keeps clear of the all-rights-reserved lightcurve table. Fails if: Fewer than ten settle in the first year, so the page is a grey bar. One survey paper delivers forty correlated settlements and they are read as forty independent tests. Only detectable periods get published (fast, high amplitude), which flatters or punishes the spin prior. Colour-based classes, which disagree with spectra for 21 to 33% of objects, are allowed to settle class predictions as if clean. Readers merge hindcast and forward marks.
- **Call it**: On an open named prediction the visitor drags their own mark onto the same track and presses keep. The call is stored with today's date in the browser and written into the page address, so a bookmark or a pasted link restores it. On return the slip shows three things on one track: the catalogue's band, their call, and the truth if it has arrived. The front page gains one line: 'You have 2 calls open; the nearest settles Apr 2028.' On the hindcast cases (size from brightness for bodies whose size was later measured) the truth sits behind a plain disclosure element, so guess-then-reveal works without scripts. Data: Nothing new for open predictions. For the hindcast cases: H and the independently measured size for the objects the science plan allows outside the sealed exam. Cost: About 150 lines of JavaScript: pointer events, encode and decode of the address fragment, storage wrapped in try/catch. No server. Fails if: Fewer than three named predictions are on file. Browser storage is purged (one major mobile browser clears it after seven days without a visit), leaving only the bookmark. The visitor does not come back for the two years a settlement takes. It is extended to guessing spectral class, which a lay reader cannot do and which turns it into a quiz.
- **The blank entry**: One page per asteroid, built as a slip. For the 94% with nothing measured it shows three predictions as bands with empty slots (size from brightness, chance of spinning faster than 2.2 hours, class base rate), each tagged 'prior only'. Under them: the reference class ('412 others at H 26.0 to 26.5 have nothing measured; of the 31 in this bin later measured, 29 spun fast'), the cheapest observation that would settle something, and the next time it can be pointed at or the sentence 'cannot be pointed at before 2046', which is true for three quarters of the list. Measured objects add a dated list of measurements with sources and conflicts, and the band before and after each one. At the foot: the issues in which anything about this object changed. Data: The ledger row with sources and conflicts, prior parameters by H bin, the measured outcomes within each H bin, window fields, and the per-object change events. Cost: About 200 lines of generator template, no JavaScript, 7,229 files with the machine record embedded in each page. Fails if: The 6,780 blank pages differ only by H and read as duplicates to people and to search engines. 'One lightcurve would settle this' is printed for an object nobody can find. The reference-class bins are too thin to show a record (only about 160 classed objects fainter than H 25).
- **Since you were last here**: The front page issue line normally reads 'issue N: 12 written, 0 settled, 1 measured, 1 window closed unobserved, 3 of 3 claims stand'. If the browser remembers an earlier visit, the same counts are summed from that issue to this one, each count a link to its items. When nothing settled it says 'nothing settled since 3 Nov'. Data: A diff of consecutive ledger snapshots keyed on object, property and source, reduced to per-issue counts and id lists, kept for every issue (about 52 rows a year). Cost: About 50 lines of JavaScript on top of the diff, which the changelog needs anyway. Fails if: The diff counts upstream reformatting as measurements, or the literature service refreshes after two months and one issue reports a flood, so the line cries wolf. With storage unavailable it falls back to the current issue, which is fine.

**Visual direction.** The one idea: blanks are drawn, not omitted. A prediction is a grey band, the truth is a mark, and where there is no truth yet there is an outlined empty slot of exactly the size the answer will occupy. The same glyph appears at every scale: the 7,229-cell grid, the bars on the due line, the rows of the record, each entry page. Most of the site is hatching and empty slots, and it fills in over years.

Ink rules. Grey hatch is belief. Paper white inside a hairline is unknown. Solid ink is a measured fact. One accent, a burnt orange, is reserved for things that have happened: the today rule, truth marks, struck claims. No red and green; a hit and a miss differ by position only, inside or outside the band, so it reads without colour.

Type. Two system stacks and no downloaded fonts: the reader's own serif for claims and prose (30 px claims, 17 px prose on a laptop; 21 and 16 on a phone), system monospace with tabular figures for every number, label, table and WRONG IF line (13 px), small uppercase letter-spaced labels at 11 px. No bold except designations.

Density. Ledger-dense: a 1,080 px column, hairline rules, 22 px table rows, 25 to 30 rows to a screen. No cards, shadows, rounded corners, icons, gradients or photographs. No pictures of asteroids: none of these bodies has been seen as more than a point of light, and the site says so once. Light paper ground by default, a near-black ground under the dark colour scheme.

Tone. Declarative and short: claim, number, WRONG IF. Misses are reported in the same voice as hits. Vocabulary is six words: claim, prediction, wrong if, due, settled, outside. No odds, stakes or payouts, which would smuggle value back in.

Motion. Nothing moves unless the visitor moves it. The feel aimed at is a world that keeps its own clock while you are away, dense panels you can read at a glance, a public ranking that starts provisional and earns its digits, and long quiet between a few large events, with the quiet composed and not filled.

**What makes it alive.** The weekly rebuild is the heartbeat, and nothing changes between rebuilds except the position of the today rule.

Week to week:
- About 12 reachable asteroids arrive each week. Each gets the frozen method's three predictions the moment it enters, by rule and with no model in the loop, so every issue writes new slips even when nothing settles.
- Each claim's WRONG IF line is restamped: 'held through issue 14 and 163 new objects', or a changed number. Claim 02 carries two counters that diverge in public: about 650 found a year against roughly 5 to 15 new rotation periods. The blank grows faster than it fills, and the site shows it growing.
- Telescope windows open and close on the line. A window that closes with no measurement becomes a permanent cross with the next chance printed, sometimes fifteen years out.
- A quiet day, which is most days, reads: 'Nothing settled this week', then what is open now and what is next, with dates. No ticker, no animation, no counter that moves without a dated fact behind it.

Year to year:
- The rule crosses the bars: Dimorphos in 2027, Kamoʻoalewa and the colour survey late in 2027, 2015 BM510 and the return of radar in 2028, Apophis in 2029.
- The grey 'could be luck' ranges on the record narrow. By my reading of the research, 10 to 40 predictions settle by the end of 2027, most in batches when a survey paper lands; that is about when the first rate is printed.
- Method versions stack. The base-rate book from release 1 is scored for as long as the site exists, and any later method is drawn on the same row against it.
- The sealed exam is opened once, at a stated gate. Fallen claims stay visible, struck through.

A returning visitor checks, in order: whether the rule has crossed anything (the record line), the since-last-visit line, their own open calls, and whether 2011 AA37 still stands.

**First release.** After gate 1 the project holds the ledger (measured values, sources, labelled priors), the orbit audit with its sensitivity, the truth table and the pre-registration. It does not hold the dynamics, the mission-type chains, calibrated forecasts or the exam. Release 1 is what those support and no more: six templates, one static figure, about 300 lines of JavaScript.

In:
- The front page as specified. The WINDOWS and SURVEYS lanes are populated; the MISSIONS slots read 'not on file yet' except the size of 2015 BM510, which the brightness prior can state.
- The base-rate book: for every object, a size band from H, a chance of spinning faster than 2.2 hours and a class base rate, frozen as method 0.1 with a hash and a DOI and labelled 'prior only'. This starts the clock on the forward record and is the baseline any later method has to beat.
- The book page: the strike table for 5, 6 and 7 km/s on the plan's fixed thresholds, the under-6 column view, and the 21-row window table with the survey-recovery flag.
- 7,229 entry pages with no script.
- The orbit claim page: one static figure of injected effect size against detection rate, the pre-registered bar, and the limits of the labelled set.

### Critique of draft section 4

- It names no sentence for the first screen. 'Uncertainty is the headline' is a stance; the findings are the orbit null and the elimination to one object, and neither appears in 4.2 as text a non-clicking visitor would read.
- The home page is an instrument whose honest output is zero or one. Under 5 km/s the count is 0 at the first gate whatever is moved, and the reward for interacting is watching the list empty. The draft's own risk 2.6 hands section 4 the job of making 'mostly prior' interesting, and a filter table does not answer it.
- The scoreboard is seventh of eight pages and appears in no phase of 4.4, yet it is the only thing that resolves in 2027 to 2029 and, by the research on how amateur results get accepted, the main source of credibility. Observe next and Dynamics are also unphased.
- Phase 1 conflicts with the science plan. It ships Known answers ('what the catalogue predicted with the truth withheld', plus a calibration curve) after step 1, while 2.3 makes the gold set a final exam and 2.5 runs it at step 3. Twelve objects do not make a curve either.
- The pages follow the work packages (ledger, audit, dynamics, shortlist, forecasts), which is the pipeline's structure and not a visitor's question. Eight page types is a lot for one maintainer, and four of them overlap.
- Nothing on the site is tied to the calendar except a release date, although the science has dated mission results and the data hold a start and end date for every observing window.
- It does not design the quiet week. 'What changed this week' will usually be about 12 new unmeasured objects and nothing else; at 5 to 15 new rotation periods a year, 'entries change as measurements land' is rare.
- It does not design the blank entry. 'A prior drawn as a wide bar, or a blank' describes 6,780 near-identical pages, and 'entries fill in over time' is untrue for most: the median arc is 12 days and only a quarter of the list can be pointed at before 2046.
- It never says how a wrong prediction is shown, what happens to old predictions when the method changes, or what counts as settled when sources disagree (6% of shared periods differ by more than 20%; colour and spectral classes disagree for 21 to 33%).
- The build budget contradicts the phasing. 4.3 promises a few hundred lines of JavaScript; four to six hand-made figures at 200 to 500 lines each is up to ten times that, placed on the latest and least certain science gate, and it is where the draft expects attention to come from.
- File-count headroom is shorter than it looks. Two files per object is 14,458 today and grows by about 1,300 a year, so the free tier's 20,000 limit arrives in about four years, sooner with issue pages.
- It does not say who the site is for. The miners said they do not want prior knowledge; the people who can act on it are observers with a telescope and researchers checking calibration, and neither is addressed by a threshold table.
- 'Reads without scripts' and 'move the thresholds and watch' are both asserted with no fallback described. It needs pre-rendered tables for a small fixed set of settings.

### Risks

- Predictions are written too late. Forecasts freeze at gate 3, but Dimorphos's mass is expected in 2027 and the Kamoʻoalewa sample late in 2027. If gate 3 slips past them, the named list shrinks to 2015 BM510 and Apophis.
- Truth dates are soft or may never come. The Tianwen-2 return date has only secondary sources, Hayabusa2 is flying on a degraded engine with a reaction-wheel fault, and 'mass expected 2027' is a year. The named record could hold one or two settlements by 2029.
- Most predictions are base rates, so the record at first scores the prior and not a method. That is defensible as a baseline but a critic will say it. Real content arrives only if the dynamics pass gate 2.
- Small numbers. For a year or more the record cannot distinguish a good forecaster from a mediocre one, and a visitor who wants a verdict gets 'provisional'.
- Settlement is noisy and selected. Only detectable periods get published, class labels from colours are wrong often enough to create false misses, and one survey paper can settle dozens at once with a shared systematic. Truth tiers and a contested state are needed from the first release.
- Naming 2011 AA37 on the front page invites reading it as the one worth mining, and its period rests on a single database field. The wording must stay 'left standing', with the WRONG IF beside it. It could fall the week after launch, which the design can show but the owner should expect.
- The lead claim can fall. The orbit null is untested in the smallest, most Earth-like corner (4 classed objects under 5 km/s), and the colour survey may fill it by end 2027. The site survives because its unit is the prediction, but the headline would be struck through in public.
- The hand-kept file of due dates and milestones is the only part that is not a deterministic rebuild. With one maintainer it will go stale unless it stays under about 12 items and the generator flags anything unchecked for 90 days.
- Betting language can read as finance and pull value back in through the side door. Keep to 'prediction' and 'settled', and print no odds.
- Registering impact-bound asteroids 'within hours' does not fit a weekly static build. Define those predictions by rule instead: whatever the frozen method gives for the first published H, computed afterwards from hashed code.
- Freeze dates have to be provable by a third party. A public commit plus a DOI before the truth date is the minimum; a hash printed on the site alone proves nothing.
- The visitor's own calls depend on browser storage that some browsers purge within a week. The address-fragment bookmark is the only durable form, and few people will keep it for two years.
